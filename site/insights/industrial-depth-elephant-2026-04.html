Field Report · Industrial Depth · 2026-04-19

Industrial depth on a free tier: 30 calls, then one model survives the day.

Fifteen free OpenRouter models, four industrial verticals, open-ended prose answers. Most models disappear after the daily free quota fires. We then ran the one survivor — openrouter/elephant-alpha, branded by OpenRouter under the literal provider name Stealth — across twelve industrial verticals. This is what we found.

What this report is

Report #2 of our Horde.Vision Insights series. We ran fifteen free models on OpenRouter against four industrial packs (battery, pharma biologics, nuclear, aerospace). After about thirty calls a global daily quota fired and fourteen of fifteen went dark — one survived, so we extended it to twelve verticals. This is a field report on what a free tier actually buys you, where it breaks, and where one non-labeled stealth slot holds up.

TL;DR
  • The cliff is real. 15 free models × 4 industrial packs = 60 cells. Only 17 succeeded. After ~30 calls, the OpenRouter "free-models-per-day" quota fires globally on the API key, taking down 14 of 15 models for the rest of the day.
  • One model survives the day. openrouter/elephant-alpha has no :free suffix and lives in a different rate-limit bucket. It is the only model to complete all four packs.
  • Industrial depth at $0. Across twelve verticals — battery, pharma, nuclear, aerospace, advanced semiconductor, defense munitions, maritime chokepoints, steel + CBAM, fertilizer + ag inputs, subsea cables, space access, grid + transformers — Elephant hit 84.9% of tier-1 markers, 34.8% of tier-2, 10.3% of tier-3, at a mean latency of 8.4 seconds. Total wall time across all 12 verticals: 96 seconds. Total spend: $0.
  • Provider literally Stealth. The OpenRouter response field provider returns the string "Stealth" on every call. Combined with the routing-alias finding from our previous report, this is a packaged product, not a model: you buy a brand, you do not learn who answered.
  • Variance is small but real. Five replicates on steel_cbam produced t3 hits of {13, 13, 14, 13, 15}. Five on pharma_biologics produced {8, 11, 9, 11, 8}. Openings of the responses were bit-for-bit identical 4 of 5 on steel and 5 of 5 on pharma. Either the routing is sticky per key, or partial response caching is in play; we cannot distinguish from the API surface.

We picked four Tier-1 industrial verticals — battery + critical minerals, pharma + biologics, nuclear fuel cycle + heavy turbines, aerospace titanium + superalloys — and wrote a short scenario plus six escalating questions for each. Open-ended prose, no schema, no function calling, temperature=0.2, max_tokens=4096. The opposite of the strict-JSON critic contract from the previous report: this batch measures industrial knowledge, not contract compliance.

Two findings worth flagging before the cliff

GLM-4.5-air recovered. In the strict-JSON critic bench, this model spent 285 seconds in hidden thinking and never returned a valid contract. With the contract removed, it lands at the very top of the depth ranking on both packs. That is a clean argument that schema-drop under thinking pressure is a contract-environment failure, not a knowledge failure.

Elephant matched the depth ceiling 24× faster. On pharma_biologics, Elephant tied glm-4.5-air at 11 / 51 tier-3 hits — at 7.1 seconds versus 176. That is not a Pareto trade-off; that is Pareto domination. On the battery pack the picture is a normal trade-off (Elephant produces fewer tier-3 hits but ten times faster than the heaviest models). On pharma the trade-off vanishes entirely.

One vertical-style flip stood out. nemotron-3-super-120b led the battery pack at t3 = 10 and collapsed to t3 = 3 on pharma — same model, same key, same prompt template, three minutes apart. A 3.3× drop in vertical depth on a single vendor's flagship is a strong argument for per-vertical benchmarking and against any "universal industrial model" ranking.

Act II — The cliff

We then ran the same fifteen models on packs three and four: nuclear_energy and aerospace_titanium. The picture changed completely. From eight successful models on pack one and seven on pack two, packs three and four each had exactly one survivor.

Why the cliff happens

OpenRouter's free tier is not "every :free model is free in unlimited quantity." It is a shared daily budget across all models that carry the :free suffix on a given API key. After roughly 30 successful calls against any combination of :free models, the provider returns: 

{
  "error": {
    "code": 429,
    "message": "Rate limit exceeded: free-models-per-day"
  }
}

This is global to the key. It does not matter which :free model you call next — they all share the same bucket. By the time pack three started, every :free model on the list had been disqualified for the rest of the day.

openrouter/elephant-alpha has no :free suffix. It is, in OpenRouter's pricing taxonomy, a separate free product hosted under a different rate-limit bucket. That is the entire reason it survives where the other fourteen do not. There is no judgment about quality embedded in this fact. The cliff is purely a contractual / billing artifact.

Practical implication. If you are designing a free-tier production system on OpenRouter, your effective model pool past the first ~30 calls per day is one model. Any architecture that assumes graceful degradation across a pool of :free alternatives is an illusion. Either upgrade to paid, or build around elephant-alpha explicitly — knowing it is a routing alias to backends you cannot name.

What Elephant knows well

On steel_cbam Elephant surfaces Vale's S11D, the Simandou JV structure (Winning + SimFer), Stegra and HYBRIT, Boston Metal MOE, Cogent / Tata as a GOES mill, the laser-scribed 0.20–0.23 mm strip grade, CBAM's default emission factors, and the 2026–2034 phase-out of free ETS allowances. That is real supply-chain literacy, not surface tagging.

On semiconductor_advanced_node it distinguishes CoWoS-S / L / R, names ASML's High-NA EXE:5000 and the EUV pellicle / mask-blank suppliers (Mitsui, HOYA, Asahi), correctly places HBM3E at SK Hynix and Micron's 2024 entry, and discusses Synopsys / Cadence export-control specifics along with Empyrean as the Chinese alternative. Twelve months ago that profile was paid-tier territory.

Where Elephant is shallow

On fertilizer_ag_inputs Elephant scores 26 of 27 tier-1 markers — essentially everyone obvious — but only 3 of 67 tier-3 markers. It does not surface Klaipeda port-denial mechanics for Belaruskali, OFAC General License numbers, Hubei Xingfa or Yunnan Yuntianhua as Chinese P producers, specific OCP Khouribga / Jorf Lasfar tonnages. The mid-tier picture is fine; the second-supplier-and-deep-policy layer is missing.

On aerospace_titanium the same shape: 24 of 27 tier-1, only 5 of 90 tier-3. Elephant knows Boeing, Airbus, CFM, P&W, Rolls-Royce, PCC, Howmet, ATI — but does not quantify VSMPO's pre-2022 share, does not name the GTF disc powder-metal contamination story, does not give shop-visit interval numbers. Knowledge is wide; quantitative recency is thin.

Some of this is marker calibration noise — the tier-3 lists are deliberately aggressive — but the directional signal is clean: Elephant is production-ready for industrial orientation, not for industrial forensics. The next post will compare these same packs against frontier paid models to calibrate the ceiling.

Variance — five replicates, two packs

We then ran Elephant five times each on the deepest vertical (steel_cbam) and a moderately deep one (pharma_biologics), with no other parameter changed. The point was to test the routing-alias hypothesis from the previous report: if Elephant routes to multiple backends, the variance should be visible across replicates.

Two unexpected things

Variance is small. On the strict-JSON critic variance series in the prior report, Elephant produced two structurally distinct output styles ("snake_case" vs prose) and a 15× latency spread. On open-ended industrial prose at temperature=0.2, the same model produced tier-3-hit variance of σ ≈ 1 marker and a latency range under 3 seconds. The bimodal output style is gone. Either the router resolves more deterministically when the request shape is "free-form prose, no schema, no tools," or temperature dampens visible style-switching enough to hide it under prose.

Openings are bit-for-bit identical. Across the five pharma_biologics replicates, all five responses began with the exact same first sentence: "1. The BIOSECURE Act of 2024 focuses on supply chain resilience for certain a…" — character for character. On steel_cbam, four of five start with "1. Iron ore: top 4 producers and their principal products"; the fifth uses an alternate phrasing. At temperature=0.2, identical openings five times in a row are extremely rare unless one of the following holds: the underlying provider is doing partial response caching, or the routing is sticky per-prompt-hash for a given key, or temperature is being clamped lower upstream than the client requested.

The provider field literally returns "Stealth" on every one of the ten variance calls. OpenRouter exposes the upstream provider name through its API; for Elephant, the upstream provider is not named. It is branded. Combined with the multi-backend finding from our previous report (under strict-JSON, Elephant produced two distinct output styles), the picture is now complete: elephant-alpha is a packaged product that fronts a pool of underlying models, sold under the brand Stealth, at $0 to the caller. You are buying a brand. You do not learn who answered.

Why this matters

For anyone building on the OpenRouter free tier

The cliff is not a quirk; it is the design. If you architect a system that assumes "free tier = pool of models I can degrade across," your effective pool past the first 30 requests of the day is one model: elephant-alpha. Plan for it explicitly. Either pay, or build the system around elephant-alpha with eyes open about what that means — a routing alias under the brand Stealth, answering in 5–13 seconds, hitting 85 % of obvious industrial entities and 10 % of expert-tier specifics.

For Insights as a product

elephant-alpha is a viable backbone for first-pass industrial orientation across many verticals on zero budget. It is not a replacement for forensic-grade reasoning, where the next post will quantify the ceiling with frontier paid flagships on the same twelve packs. But for the question "what does the field look like in this vertical, and what should I read next," the data above says it works.

For LLM benchmarking culture

Public leaderboards record per-call performance: speed, quality, cost. They do not record provider-side rate-limit budgets, daily quota cliffs, sticky routing, or the difference between a named model and a brand-fronted routing alias. Each of those is a load-bearing fact for production systems. None of them shows up on Artificial Analysis or LMSYS leaderboards. We expect this gap to widen as more "free" model offerings appear and as routing aliases become a standard provider product.

Methodology

Packs
12 packs total. Each is a 3–5 sentence industrial scenario plus 6 escalating questions in plain English — no schema, no function calling, no tools. Every pack is a single prompt.md file paired with its rubric. Available on request.
Marker rubric
For each pack, a sibling markers.yaml lists tier-1 / tier-2 / tier-3 knowledge markers, written ahead of the run. The model never sees this file. Scoring is case-insensitive substring match. Conservative on purpose — no LLM-as-judge in v0. (See the glossary for tier definitions.)
Models
Four-pack comparative batch: 15 free-tier OpenRouter models, identical to the strict-JSON bench from the April report, minus models that have churned. Twelve-pack stealth-player batch: openrouter/elephant-alpha only. Variance batch: openrouter/elephant-alpha at n=5 on two packs.
Sampling
temperature=0.2, max_tokens=4096, timeout=240s, single retry on 429 / 5xx with 4-second backoff.
Harness
A single-purpose Python script that takes a list of models and a list of packs, sends the prompts, and writes one JSONL row per response. Captures per call: latency, input / output tokens, finish reason, OpenRouter provider field, raw response text, and tier-1 / 2 / 3 marker hit counts and examples. Available on request.
Cost & wall time
Four-pack 15-model run: 29 minutes, $0. Eight-pack Elephant solo: 73 seconds, $0. Variance probe (10 calls): 86 seconds, $0. Total: ≈ 32 minutes, $0.
Interpreter
Human-written from the raw run summaries. v1 will move this to a fixed model + fixed prompt with a published SHA per the Insights vision document §7.

What we did not measure

  • Hallucination rate. Marker hits show what was named; they do not check whether the named entities were named correctly. Spot-checks suggest most named entities are real, but a systematic factual-claim audit is future work.
  • Head-to-head with paid frontier models on the same twelve packs. Deferred to the next report; the cliff would not even appear there because paid models have separate billing.
  • Variance on more than two packs. The twelve-vertical depth is n=1 per pack. Variance σ on the two probed packs is ≈ 1 marker; we assume the others sit in the same regime, but have not measured.
  • Role-fit benchmarks. This batch is "knowledge probe" only. Critic / canonicalizer / supervisor role-fit lives in a separate program; see the Insights vision document.
  • Hallucination patterns specific to fertilizer_ag_inputs, where the tier-3 collapse may indicate either knowledge gap or marker calibration issue. We have not yet split the two.
